The concept of blogging has also been something that I've wanted to get into for a while now. Joining the startup world was personally kind of difficult for me. It wasn't that I was technically incompetent or something; it was more that I just had such a hard time navigating the rocky start that most startups face. That's why I want this article to be my own documentation of the whole process. It's something that I hope I can read back on one day and enjoy telling people the story of!

## Beginnings

I wasn't always a startup founder. In fact, I was kind of the opposite. In my mind, basically the only real path to gaining stability in life was to go to college, learn how to play with computers, get a job at FAANG or whatever people say is cool, and be happy with that. Startups were the exact opposite - literally anything could go wrong, and while the reward can be insanely high, the risk is even crazier. I used to clown on startup founders who thought they were so cool for building software instead of working for a billion-dollar corporation, something that I regret doing to this day.

### Applying to the Hatchery

It's welcome week. Sophomore year has just started, no classes have started, and a ping on your phone says that everyone's meeting up at the dining hall in 15. You throw on a pair of nice pants and a T-shirt, put your headphones in, and throw on some music. As you're scrolling through Instagram, your next-door suitemate comes in with a crazy idea: let's build a startup! 

Apparently, the internship he worked at during the summer had him building these IoT networks using a long-range communication protocol (I won't go into too much detail since this isn't meant to be technical). He wants to take these networks that he's built and apply them to real estate. Install sensors and monitor buildings, and then have a system report whenever something goes wrong. There aren't many companies that do this, and the ones that do are outdated and kind of expensive. 

There's potential.

Next week, there's going to be a kick-off event for this program called the Mohktarzada Hatchery. They'd give you $10K and the resources you need to build a startup from the ground-up. The guys who run it were the people who built Rocket Money, valued at almost $1.5B. This was the real deal, so you eagerly accept his deal and plan to attend next week.

Next week comes. You listen to their presentation, and at the end you go out to the networking event. After you both overcome the anxiety and stress of talking to someone rich (it doesn't help that you're both computer science majors), you pitch the idea to three of the four mentors. 

Would they like it? What issues would they have? Would they turn down the idea instantly? After all, during the Q&A held at the presentation, they'd just flamed the person sitting next to you two for building a school marketplace app. These brothers were ruthless and would call out anything bad about an idea.

So you walk up... and tell them. As you gauge their reactions, you're surprised to see interest and excitement in their eyes. "This is great! Actually, the other day, one of my properties had a leak and I'd really have appreciated if I had a monitoring service like your idea installed. You should totally apply!" 

Wait, they liked it??

"Oh, by the way, what's your company name?"

What was your company name? You were so engulfed in our idea that the only time you had to come up company names was right before, when your roommate used ChatGPT while we got ready. And in the moment, one name came to mind.

"SenseGuard. We're building SenseGuard."

This is exactly what happened to me, and how we got our first push to building. A quirky video later, and some quick answers to some simple questions, we handed in our application. Three weeks later, we received an email that we were 1 of 4 startups chosen to build with the Hatchery.

### Early Game

*As a disclaimer, I'm not going to get technical in this article. You can go to LinkedIn and read posts from your local AI thought leader if you're angry about that.*

On our first day of Hatchery, we got to meet all of the mentors, people we had already talked with at the kickoff event that we were lucky to meet again. Things got down to business, and after we chose our seats we were already given our first task.

"Make a presentation with three slides. The first slide should have what you worked on for the past 2 weeks, the second slide has what you plan on doing in the next 2 weeks, and the last slide should have blockers and challenges you expect to face. Be harsh and critical on yourself."

What? I was so confused about this. Honestly, it felt kind of unnecessary. Why did I have to do it in this order? Why couldn't I just talk in front of them for a bit and explain what I've been up to informally? Did we really have to do all of this pomp and circumstance? Welp, this wasn't the time to be asking questions. Annoyed, I worked on the slides with my partner and we presented. 

"Ok, so this slide is really vague and honestly, it feels like you're just throwing buzzwords at me."

Wow, these guys were really brutal. They disassembled the slides that we wrote seamlessly, finding issue with almost everything written on it. It was harsh, a direct hit to my ego. It wasn't often that your work got absolutely destroyed by a bunch of rich guys, anyways. Looking back, this was definitely needed. I realize that part of me was just trying to impress them with all of the stuff we did to prepare for hatchery, and that in the end it didn't really matter to them. They cared more about actual goals and things I did, not buzzwords and crazy words meant to seduce them into falling in love with our startup. 

## The Hatchery

With the Hatchery came a lot more than just the mentorship and the money. For one, we got to meet our cohort and see what their ideas were. ThinkEx, Capy's Journey, and Cube were all really cool people, and very talented startup founders. Having them around to keep us motivated and pushing to build better things was really important.

Another perk was having a space within our computer science building to work. It was a cozy room on the second floor of the Brendan Iribe building. 10 standing desks were scattered around the space with some of the comfiest chairs I've felt in a while. In the corner, there was a sink with a Keurig and coffee pods stocked. There was a cart filled with snacks (although it was only really filled for the first week, we ate all of it pretty quickly). In the other corner was a refrigerator where we chugged through soda like there was no tomorrow. 

Having a dedicated space to work in was a lot more impactful than I thought it would be. I also quickly realized that I hated doing homework in my room (something I wish I know in freshman year). Having a place where I knew I could find my partner working was so nice to have, and it quickly became our HQ where we put various things like a Raspberry Pi, our gateways and sensor prototypes, and taco bell sauce packs to play basketball with in some baskets we found. It eventually became routine for my partner and I to wake up, go to classes and then straight to the Hatchery. 

We also found ourselves becoming really close friends with [ThinkEx](https://thinkex.app). Not only did we find ourselves using their product a lot (subtle ad, I know), but we also showed them everything we were working on and got to help them test out their product when they eventually rolled out to customers. Having such close friends who were more locked in than we could even dream to be was a ton of fun and I can say that this was the reason why I started to love building startups.

## Advice

So I've been building for like 9 months now. By the time I'm writing this article, Hatchery has ended and we're currently in summer vacation. Compared to a lot of other people in the startup world, I'm like a baby (quite fitting since it's been 9 months). What I think I do have though is a deep interest in tech that, as a college student, I can keep up with better than people with, y'know, actual jobs. 

My first piece is that age doesn't actually matter much. In fact, for me, it feels kind of like a flex. It's actually kind of astonishing how many people I've met that are so interested in startups, but don't actually want to make one since they think they're too young. Age doesn't matter in the startup world (don't take this out of context)! Even if we were to put away emotions and look at this logically (fitting for a computer science major), you'll realize that no one actually cares that you're young. As long as you can serve the customer in a way that makes them happy, they don't care who they're giving their money away to. If your startup fails, no worries! Just start a new one, it's not like you have a family to take care of. You can afford to make mistakes at a young age.

My second piece is to have a co-founder. This actually felt really simple to me, since I've always worked with a partner. However, I didn't realize how many people actually didn't work with a co-founder and were just balling out. I guarantee that work becomes a lot easier and fun with a partner. I don't think I could imagine SenseGuard being where it's at without mine. When you start doing almost 6-8 hours of work everyday, it can help to have a cofounder, especially when you can get along with them really well and be friends outside of work (F1 reference???). Everyone recommends having a co-founder, and some accelerators (like Hatchery) even require you to have a team!

Lastly, have consistent goals and accountability. I bet literally everyone has heard this before, though, which is why I recommend using the slide structure SenseGuard used. It's simple, and will set you on track. 

Here it is again for reference, with a couple of improvements:

1. On the first slide, paste in what you said you would do the 2 weeks prior. If this is your first time doing it, then don't worry about this slide.
2. On the second slide, write down what you were actually able to get done within the past 2 weeks. Be really descriptive or honest with yourself. You might find yourself glancing at the first side and being frustrated that you couldn't finish something - that's totally fine.
3. On the third slide, describe what you'll get done in the next 2 weeks. Be VERY descriptive and make realistic goals. Use precise numbers and be honest with yourself. This will be your template for the next 2 weeks, so be sure you put in a lot of thought when making it.
4. On the last slide, talk about your blockers and challenges. Once again, be descriptive and honest. Try and make these realistic as well, and issues you're actually having in the present and can solve in the near future. Don't add long-term blockers unless they're REALLY impactful.

Show this to your friends in the startup space and whatnot! If you're an accelerator, I'd recommend asking your people to try this out. It's kind of like what people doing the YC app feel: it makes you note down challenges and issues you're having, and outlines clearly where you want to see the business. Instead of the next 3 months, like with YC, this just does it for the next 2 weeks. Make this a routine, and I guarantee you'll see improved results.
